Avow lets a verified business give its AI agents the authority to make commitments, each backed by money reserved in the business's own lock for one named supplier. The supplier checks who stands behind the agent, what it may commit to and the money backing it, with open-source code and the chain, so two agents that have never met can rely on each other.

Agents can already pay. Most trade between businesses runs on promises made before anyone pays in full: a reservation, a booking, a purchase order, an agreement to pay later. A supplier holding GPUs for next Tuesday needs to know the agent booking them can bind a real business, stays within limits that business set, and has money set aside if it cancels late. Avow gives the agent all three.

Avow runs on Tempo's Moderato testnet with test dollars. Reserves are funded by the buyer today, and other kinds of backing come later.

## From authority to commitment

A business's administrator approves an agent with a passkey, under a mandate that names the suppliers it may book, the most per commitment, the most held at once and an end date. The agent's key also carries a daily limit on Tempo. Three layers hold the agent to those limits. Avow's API refuses a reserve the mandate doesn't allow, the lock takes a reserve only with Avow's approval over the exact terms, and Tempo's keychain refuses what the key can't move.

Each reserve backs one commitment for one named supplier, in the business's own lock. The terms say the most the supplier can charge, for a late cancellation or a no-show, and when the reserve goes back.

Take a GPU booking with a late-cancellation charge of up to \$500. The business reserves \$500, and the supplier checks the commitment before it accepts. Usage is paid separately. Once no charge can apply, the reserve returns to the business, and a charge that does apply follows the agreed process, with a window to contest it.

Revoking an agent stops its new commitments. Commitments it already made keep their terms.

## Composable commitments

A business's promise often rests on another business keeping its own. A compute reseller sells 8 H100s to a lab and books the same capacity from a GPU cloud, so its sale stands on its own purchase.

A commitment can name the commitments it relies on, by chain, lock, commitment id and terms hash, in [version 3 terms](/docs/composable-commitments/terms-v3). Before it relies on one, a business checks it against the chain: a lock Avow's factory made, owned by the business offering, accepted by its supplier, under matching terms and covering the same capacity. The buyer runs that check before funding, a reseller's verifier runs it before accepting, and Avow's API runs it before approving the buyer's reserve, refusing with the same reason codes. [Checking dependencies](/docs/composable-commitments/dependency-check) covers each test and what's still to come.

Each link stays its own commitment between its two businesses, with its own reserve. The reseller answers to the lab whatever happens upstream, no money moves between locks, and a check proves only that the upstream booking was accepted when it was checked.

## Verification and settlement

The open verifier checks signatures, the agreed terms and the backing on chain without calling Avow. Who the business is, and Avow's approval decisions, still rest on Avow, and a sandbox business isn't verified.

Settlement follows each commitment's terms. The reserve releases when the supplier's exposure ends, a charge is paid after its contest window, and a contested charge pauses for a ruling under the agreed dispute process. [Trust model](/docs/get-started/trust-model) sets out who holds which key and what the lock holds Avow to.
